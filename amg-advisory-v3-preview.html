01 · Process discovery and redesign

Fix the work before you automate it.

The problem. Work slows down between departments, not inside them. Handovers, approvals sitting in one inbox, the same question asked three times.

What we do. We map how work actually moves rather than how the procedure says it does, then separate the steps worth automating from the steps worth deleting. Automating a bad process only makes the error arrive faster.

The outcome. Shorter cycle times and fewer exceptions before a system is built — and a cheaper build when it comes.

Planning assumption: 10–30% cycle-time reduction from process change alone3–5 weeks

What you get

  • Current-state process map
  • Time and cost per step
  • Exception and rework analysis
  • Prioritised change list

02 · Document and invoice processing

Arrival to ledger, without retyping.

The problem. Documents arrive in every format and by every channel. Someone reads each one, decides what it is, and types its contents into a system that could have received it directly.

The outcome. Administrative hours returned, a complete audit trail, and a month-end that stops being an event.

How we work

A method designed so you can stop at any point.

Most consultancy risk comes from committing to an outcome before anyone has measured the problem. Each phase is short, separately priced, and leaves you with something of independent value.

Placeholder[IMAGE REQUIRED — close-up of physical filing or invoice batch, dark exposure, wide crop]
01

Discover

2–4 weeks

We spend time inside the operation.

We sit with the people doing the work and follow each process from first trigger to last handover, recording every manual step, exception and workaround. Then we put numbers against it: hours per month, cost per transaction, error rate, time spent waiting.

You keep the process map and the baseline, whether or not you continue.

02

Design

1–2 weeks

We decide what is worth changing.

Not everything should be automated. We rank opportunities by hours returned against effort and risk, design the target process, and agree how success will be measured before a line of code exists.

A prioritised plan, with fixed scope and fixed price per phase.

03

Build

4–12 weeks

We build in short cycles, in your environment.

Systems are built around the tools you already run rather than replacing them. You see working software in the first fortnight and every fortnight after, so direction can change while changing it is still cheap.

Working software, your infrastructure, your accounts, your code.

04

Deploy

1–2 weeks

The old process keeps running until the new one has earned its place.

We run both in parallel and compare outputs until the numbers agree. Training happens inside the team's own workflow, not in a room, and every deployment keeps a documented manual fallback.

A live system, a trained team, and a way back.

05

Improve

Ongoing, optional

We report against the baseline we agreed at the start.

Every month we compare performance to the Discovery baseline and act on the gap. When there is nothing useful left to do, we say so and stop, rather than finding new reasons to stay.

A short monthly report, including the parts that did not work.

Illustrative operational cases

Five process models, worked through end to end.

AMG Advisory is a new consultancy. There are no client case studies to show, and inventing them would be the fastest way to lose the trust this site is built on. What follows is how we think: five common process shapes, each taken through the same analysis we would apply to yours.

Finance

400 monthly supplier invoices

Illustrative model

Current process

Invoices arrive by email, supplier portal and post. One person opens each, matches it to a purchase order by hand, keys it into the ERP, routes it for approval, chases the approver, and files a copy.

Operational friction

Six manual steps per invoice, three systems, and an approval stage that waits on individual inboxes. Errors surface at month end, when correcting them is most expensive.

How we would measure it

Time twenty real invoices end to end, including the ones that went wrong. Record touches, elapsed time, and the proportion that failed to match first time.

Possible intervention

Automated intake across all three channels, line-level matching against purchase orders, approval routing with escalation, and a single exception queue.

Metrics that would need verifying

Invoices processed without a manual touch; days from receipt to approval; cost per invoice; discount capture rate; first-time match rate.

Risks and exceptions

Non-standard supplier formats, credit notes, partial deliveries, and purchase orders raised after the invoice arrives. Each needs a defined path before go-live.

What a successful result would look like

The majority of invoices complete without a person, the exception queue is small enough to clear daily, and close is no longer gated by unmatched items.

Finance

Monthly management reporting

Illustrative model

Current process

Figures are exported from several systems, reconciled in a spreadsheet, formatted by one person who knows the template, and circulated with commentary written last.

Operational friction

Three to five finance days per cycle, a single-person dependency on the template, and departments arriving at meetings with different numbers.

How we would measure it

Record elapsed hours per close over two cycles, by activity: extraction, reconciliation, formatting, commentary, distribution.

Possible intervention

Automated collection and reconciliation from each source, variance detection, and a drafted commentary the analyst edits rather than writes.

Metrics that would need verifying

Finance days consumed per close; working days from period end to issue; number of restatements after issue; number of competing figures in circulation.

Risks and exceptions

Source systems that disagree by design, late adjustments, and definitions of the same metric that differ between departments. Definitions must be agreed before automation, not after.

What a successful result would look like

The pack issues in one day from one agreed set of numbers, and the analyst's time moves from assembly to interpretation.

Operations

Shared customer-service inbox

Illustrative model

Current process

A shared mailbox receives a high volume of repetitive enquiries. Staff read each one, decide who it belongs to, look up an answer, and reply.

Operational friction

Triage consumes more time than answering. Response times vary by who is on duty, and there is no reliable record of what was asked most often.

How we would measure it

Categorise a fortnight of real messages. Record volume by type, time to first response, and the share answerable from existing documented sources.

Possible intervention

Automated classification and routing, drafted replies for the highest-volume categories, and a review step before anything is sent.

Metrics that would need verifying

Time to first response; share auto-classified correctly; share of drafts sent without material edit; reopen rate.

Risks and exceptions

Complaints, vulnerable customers and anything with legal implications must be routed to a person by rule, not by classifier confidence.

What a successful result would look like

Routine enquiries answer quickly and consistently, and staff time concentrates on the cases that genuinely need a person.

Knowledge

Contract and policy retrieval

Illustrative model

Current process

Answers live in signed contracts, policy documents and project folders. People ask a colleague because searching is slower than asking.

Operational friction

Interruptions concentrate on a small number of experienced staff. Answers vary by who is asked, and nothing is traceable to a source.

How we would measure it

Log questions asked of the three most-interrupted people for two weeks: volume, category, time to answer, and whether the answer existed in a document.

Possible intervention

An internal assistant grounded strictly in approved documents, with permissions mirroring the organisation and a citation on every answer.

Metrics that would need verifying

Questions answered without a colleague; citation accuracy on a sampled set; interruption volume for the named individuals; time to answer.

Risks and exceptions

Superseded document versions, permission boundaries, and confident answers drawn from outdated policy. Version control has to be resolved first.

What a successful result would look like

Staff self-serve most routine questions with a traceable source, and senior interruptions fall measurably.

Operations

Multi-system customer onboarding

Illustrative model

Current process

A new customer is set up across a CRM, a finance system and an operational platform, with documents collected by email and checks recorded in a spreadsheet.

Operational friction

The same details are entered three times, status is invisible between teams, and onboarding stalls without anyone noticing until the customer asks.

How we would measure it

Track twenty onboardings end to end: elapsed days, touches per case, re-entry count, and where each one waited longest.

Possible intervention

One intake point feeding all three systems, automated document chasing, and a shared status view with alerts when a case stops moving.

Metrics that would need verifying

Elapsed days to fully onboarded; re-entry count per case; proportion stalled beyond an agreed threshold; error rate at first billing.

Risks and exceptions

Regulatory checks that must remain human, incomplete customer submissions, and systems without a usable interface. Each constrains the design.

What a successful result would look like

Details are entered once, status is visible to everyone who needs it, and stalled cases surface automatically rather than on complaint.

Questions

The things asked before committing.

Do we need our data in order before we start?

No. Data quality is the most common reason given for postponing this kind of work. Discovery establishes what state things are actually in, and where data quality genuinely blocks an opportunity we say so and price that work separately rather than hiding it inside a build.

What does an engagement cost?

Discovery is a fixed fee based on the number of processes in scope and typically runs two to four weeks. Build phases are quoted individually once scope is agreed, so you always know the cost of the next step before committing to it. We do not work on open-ended time and materials.

How do you handle data protection and GDPR?

We work inside your existing infrastructure and data residency requirements. Where a system processes personal data we document the lawful basis, minimise what is retained, and provide the technical detail your DPO needs for the processing record. Where an EU or Norwegian hosting requirement applies, we architect for it rather than around it.

We already have Microsoft 365 and Copilot. Do we need anything else?

Possibly not, and we will tell you when a licence you already hold covers the need. General assistants help individuals work faster. They do not remove a process, because they do not connect your systems, enforce your rules or carry an audit trail. That is the gap this work is aimed at.

Will this cost people their jobs?

That decision is yours, and you should make it deliberately rather than discover it. The honest pattern in this kind of work is that repetitive administrative tasks disappear and the roles built around them change. The organisations this suits are usually trying to absorb growth without hiring. We will not pretend the question does not exist.

What happens when the system gets something wrong?

It is designed to be wrong safely. Every system has confidence thresholds, an exception queue, a named owner, and a logged decision trail. Cases the system is not certain about go to a person by design, and accuracy is reported monthly rather than when asked.

Do we need technical people internally?

Not to run what we build. You need one person who can make decisions about the process and one who can approve access. If you have an IT function we work with them; if you do not, we handle the environment and hand it over documented.

You are a new firm. Why should we take the risk?

Because the engagement is structured so that you do not have to take much of one. Start with Discovery: a short, fixed-price piece of analysis that leaves you with a process map, a cost baseline and a ranked list of opportunities, all of which have value even if you never work with us again. Judge the thinking before you commit to a build.

About

A deliberately narrow practice.

We founded AMG Advisory because operational problems are rarely solved by adding another piece of software. The work combines business analysis, process design and modern engineering to build systems around how organisations actually operate.

We will not pretend otherwise, and the engagement is designed so you do not have to take our word for anything.

The premise is simple. The technology is rarely the hard part. The hard part is understanding a business well enough to know which work should exist at all — and that requires spending time inside an operation rather than reading a description of it.

So we built the practice we would have wanted to hire. Fixed scope and fixed price. A short, honest piece of analysis first, so the decision to proceed rests on evidence rather than enthusiasm. And a firm limit on how many engagements run at once, because the quality of this work depends entirely on how well the operation underneath it is understood.

How we operate

Measure first

Fit the organisation

Systems are designed around how the work is actually done, workarounds included. A process that exists only in the handbook is not a process.

03

Say the unwelcome thing

If an idea will not pay for itself, if the timing is wrong, or if the constraint is organisational rather than technical, you hear it from us early.

04

Leave nothing behind

No proprietary layer, no hidden dependency, no locked accounts. Our value is the next problem we solve, not the last one we are holding.
